\section{Introduction}
Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is among the most heavily tested psychotherapies. A meta-analysis of 41 placebo-controlled trials in anxiety and related disorders (total $N=2{,}843$) found that CBT outperformed placebo on target symptoms with a moderate effect (Hedges' $g=0.56$) \citep{carpenter2018cbt}. The same literature also shows a ceiling: across 87 studies, only about half of patients respond to CBT for anxiety disorders, and the figure depends heavily on how response is defined \citep{loerinc2015response}. A therapy can work on average and still leave open \emph{why} it works and for \emph{whom}.

The textbook account of CBT's cognitive component is a top-down loop. A situation triggers an automatic thought (``I am going to fail this talk''); the patient records it, names the distortion, weighs evidence for and against, and generates a more balanced thought; the feeling is expected to follow. This review asks a narrow question about that loop: \emph{does the mechanism ask the patient to use a capacity that anxiety, and in some patients ADHD, have already degraded?} If so, the cognitive component should work best when practiced calm, should depend on baseline executive capacity, and should contribute less to outcome than behavioural elements that do not demand effortful reasoning.

\paragraph{Scope and method} This is a critical (theoretical) review. It does not claim that CBT is ineffective, and it does not present a new systematic search of all CBT trials. It selects the literatures needed to evaluate one mechanistic argument, states what each can and cannot show, and lists what we could not find. All citations were checked against the publisher record (DOI resolved through Crossref) and, for numerical claims, against the abstract or full text; the search and verification record is in the repository (\texttt{deep-research/}). Claims we could not source are marked in the text. Popular statements of the argument, notably by Judson Brewer \citep{brewer2016habit,brewer2021unwinding,brewer2026cbt}, are treated as claims to be evaluated, not as evidence.
