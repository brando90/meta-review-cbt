\section{Therapist, delivery and real-world factors}\label{sec:delivery}
\paragraph{Therapists} Therapist effects are real but modest: about 5\% of outcome variance on average (range 0.2--29\%; \citealp{johns2019therapist}), 6--7\% among low-intensity workers in IAPT \citep{firth2015pwp}, and in one large routine dataset individual therapists' recovery rates ranged from 23.5\% to 95.6\% \citep{saxon2012therapist}.
Whether delivering CBT competently or adherently matters is unsettled: a meta-analysis found adherence--outcome and competence--outcome correlations near zero ($r=.02$ and $.07$; modalities pooled; \citealp{webb2010adherence}), while single CBT studies find competence effects mostly at the extremes or in subgroups \citep{branson2015competence,strunk2010competence,kuyken2008competence}.
Experience alone did not improve outcomes across 6,591 patients and 170 therapists \citep{goldberg2016experience}.
The therapeutic alliance includes agreement on goals and tasks and a therapeutic bond.
A synthesis of 295 studies found a face-to-face alliance--outcome association of $r=.278$ (95\% CI $[.256,.299]$), with a similar association for internet therapy \citep{fluckiger2018alliance}.
This is correlational and not CBT-specific.
In one sample of 331 patients seen by 80 therapists, therapist-level but not patient-level alliance variation predicted outcome \citep{baldwin2007alliance}.
Session-by-session data from 17 studies and 5,350 participants showed reciprocal relations between early alliance and symptoms, rather than a simple one-way account \citep{fluckiger2020reciprocal}.

\paragraph{Drift and training} Reviews argue that clinicians under-use core behavioural components and drift from ``doing'' to ``talking'' therapy \citep{waller2009drift,waller2016driftredux}; a survey found most licensed psychologists did not use exposure for PTSD \citep{becker2004exposure} (dated, US).
We found no pooled prevalence of drift.
Training changes behaviour only when it is active and supervised: unsupervised internet training did not change competence in a randomized trial \citep{rakovshik2016supervision}, in line with broader reviews \citep{beidas2010training,shafran2009gap}.

\paragraph{Dose, homework and schedule} Homework compliance correlates with outcome ($r=.26$, 23 studies; \citealp{mausbach2010homework}; $g=0.79$ for quantity, \citealp{kazantzis2016quality}), but patients who improve may do more homework.
Improvement is curvilinear with dose, and most responders in routine care respond within 7 (low-intensity) or 14 (high-intensity) sessions \citep{robinson2020dose}; session frequency may matter more than total sessions \citep{cuijpers2013sessions}.
Intensified exposure shortened time to response in a randomized trial; its authors reported formally equivalent symptom reduction at post-treatment and six months \citep{pittig2021intensified}.

\paragraph{Does CBT survive contact with routine care?} Largely yes.
Effectiveness studies for anxiety lie within the range of efficacy trials \citep{Stewart2009Anxiety}, those for depression are somewhat lower with about 25\% dropout \citep{Hans2013Depression}, and clinical representativeness did not erase effects \citep{shadish2000representative}.
IAPT shows pre--post $d$ of 0.87 (depression) and 0.88 (anxiety) \citep{wakefield2021iapt}, though year-one reliable recovery was 40.3\% \citep{Gyani2013IAPT}, and a small independent diagnostic follow-up found only 9.2\% fully recovered (n = 90; single author; contested; \citealp{scott2018iapt}).
Youth evidence-based practices beat usual care only modestly (mean effect 0.29; \citealp{weisz2013youthusual}).

\paragraph{Format and personalisation} A synthesis of 20 studies with 1,418 participants found a small average difference between guided internet and face-to-face CBT ($g=0.05$, 95\% CI $[-0.09,0.20]$), with limited evidence for individual conditions \citep{carlbring2018icbt}.
Guided internet CBT works in routine care \citep{etzelmueller2020routine}, and guided beats unguided only slightly at post-treatment \citep{karyotaki2021icbtnma}; unguided CBT has a small effect ($g=0.27$; \citealp{karyotaki2017selfguided}).
A large pragmatic trial of supported computerised CBT in primary care found no benefit over usual care, with low uptake \citep{gilbody2015reeact}.
Group and individual formats showed little average difference when treatments, patients and doses were matched \citep{burlingame2016group}.
\citet{truijens2019manual} found no support for manual superiority in six direct comparisons, but three of eight indirect meta-analyses favoured it.
These findings do not establish general superiority of manualised care, nor does the absence of superiority establish equivalence.
Algorithm-matched care modestly beat stepped care (OR 1.40; \citealp{delgadillo2022stratified}; see also \citealp{firth2015stepped}).

\paragraph{Relationship, feedback and timing}
Repairing alliance ruptures was associated with better outcomes across 11 studies ($r=.29$, 95\% CI $[.10,.47]$), but six training/supervision comparisons did not establish an outcome benefit ($r=.11$, $[-.09,.30]$) \citep{eubanks2018rupture}.
A cluster-randomized trial provides more direct evidence for structured feedback: among 2,233 patients, those not on track improved more when therapists received progress feedback \citep{Delgadillo2018Feedback}.
This supports monitoring and adaptation, not the claim that every informal therapeutic probe is effective.

The concern that an early reframe may feel invalidating is a plausible clinical hypothesis, but the cited alliance and reactance findings do not directly test that timing mechanism.
Relational readiness must also be distinguished from whether restructuring occurs before or after exposure.
In 93 participants with claustrophobic fear, restructuring before exposure and consolidation after exposure produced no detected difference in outcomes through one month \citep{krause2022claustrophobia}.
The cognitive exercises differed as well as their order, and the trial was not an equivalence test.
The public-speaking-anxiety trial supports explicit expectancy processing before or after exposure, with a small, non-significant anxiety advantage and greater expectancy change in the after-exposure condition (Section~\ref{sec:ingredients}).
Alliance, communication and timing may condition a technique's usefulness.

The informal clinical perspective proposes a sequence in which experiential and emotional change precede a final phase of \emph{cognitive consolidation}: deliberate reflection on what changed and how to sustain it.
It also allows earlier reframing when that helps a person engage in a different experience.
This is a proposed therapeutic sequence, not a universal rule established by the timing trials above.
Consolidation after an individual exposure and consolidation near the end of therapy are different timing questions.

\paragraph{Reading} R9 is partly supported.
CBT works in routine care with variable yield, and some delivery changes, including structured feedback, have causal evidence.
How much non-response is attributable to delivery rather than treatment content or patient/context factors remains unresolved.
