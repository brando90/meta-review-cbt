\title{Reasoning Your Way Out of Anxiety? A Critical Review of Cognitive Restructuring, Executive Function and Adult ADHD}

\author{Brando Miranda\corref{cor1}}
\ead{brando9@stanford.edu}
\cortext[cor1]{Corresponding author}
\address{Stanford University, Stanford, CA, USA}

\begin{abstract}
Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is the best-studied psychotherapy for anxiety, yet only about half of treated patients respond, and the mechanism usually offered for its cognitive component, that patients change feelings by examining and replacing automatic thoughts, has seldom been tested against the cognitive demands it places on them. We ask whether cognitive restructuring is an executive-function task that anxiety and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) make harder, and whether it is the part of CBT that carries the benefit. We specify the executive demands of restructuring, review evidence on stress, anxiety and prefrontal function (including a check of the popular ``prefrontal cortex goes off-line'' claim against its sources), review efficacy, bias and component evidence, review alternatives and CBT in adult ADHD, and assess the sparse direct evidence on executive function as a moderator of outcome. About 300 sources were DOI-verified. The evidence supports a narrow claim and not a broad one. Reappraisal draws on working memory in the laboratory, but evidence that stress abolishes it is contested. CBT is effective on average, though effects shrink with active controls and bias adjustment and have not grown over three decades. Component studies, though underpowered, repeatedly fail to find added benefit from restructuring over exposure or behavioural activation. Executive function predicts uptake of the cognitive skill in older adults more than overall outcome. In adult ADHD, effective CBT protocols look behavioural, and we found no study of ADHD as a moderator of CBT for anxiety. We close with preregistrable predictions and a research agenda. \textbf{Draft 2; for the author's review.}
\end{abstract}
