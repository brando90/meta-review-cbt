# CBT and its limits: paper outline and evidence search (draft 1, Oct 25 2026)

**Status:** working draft for Brando's review. Public-repo safe: no names of private people, no personal health details. The five points that started this came from an informal personal communication; they are treated as hypotheses, not as evidence, and are never cited. Every citation below was checked to exist (Crossref DOI match on author, year, venue, title; abstract read where noted). Anything I could not verify is listed as dropped. Numbers come from abstracts or full text I read, not from memory.

**Labels:** Supported / Partly supported / Contested / Not supported / No evidence found. Each source line has a verdict for the specific claim: SUPPORTS / DOES NOT SUPPORT / MIXED.

## 1. Bottom line (read this first)

**C1. A large share of CBT's measured effect against waitlist or no treatment comes from attention, time, contact and expectancy, not from the cognitive technique**

- Label: **Partly supported**
- Why: Psychological-placebo arms beat waitlist by about half an SD (Faltinsen 2022, SMD -0.49); structurally equal placebos erase the specific advantage (Baskin 2003). But CBT still beats placebo (Carpenter g = 0.56). Contact time and session number do not predict effect size (Cuijpers 2013). Expressive writing and venting effects are tiny (r .075, d .19), so "venting" alone cannot explain CBT effects

**C2. Researcher allegiance inflates the CBT-versus-other comparisons**

- Label: **Supported in general; weak for CBT specifically**
- Why: Allegiance and outcome correlate (Munder 2013, r = .262, 30 meta-analyses; Dragioti 2015, ROR 1.31). But Dragioti found no significant allegiance effect for CBT, and Tolin 2010's CBT advantage survived adjustment. Allegiance cuts both ways

**C3. Attempted solutions (forcing sleep, suppression, avoidance, safety behaviours) perpetuate problems**

- Label: **Partly supported**
- Why: Mechanism is real but modest: suppression rebound small to moderate (Abramowitz 2001) and not larger in clinical groups (Magee 2012); reducing safety behaviours d = 0.43, increasing them d = 0.65 (Goodson 2026). It is also a core CBT formulation (Salkovskis; Harvey 2002), so it argues for a CBT component, not against CBT

**C4. Therapy that interrupts the attempted solution (strategic / Palo Alto therapy, paradoxical intention) resolves problems at least as well as CBT, faster**

- Label: **Contested / thin**
- Why: Paradoxical intention works for insomnia but equals stimulus control and relaxation (Turner & Ascher 1979; Jansson-Frojmark 2022, about 10 trials, none recent). Pure strategic therapy: uncontrolled in-house case series, one small RCT vs CBT (Jackson 2018, n = 60, advocate-run). No independent trial found. Nardone's 80 to 95 percent success figures are in-house and not used

**C5. Reason does not change behaviour; emotion does**

- Label: **Contested** (strong form: Not supported)
- Why: Experimental meta-analyses show changing intentions and attitudes changes behaviour (Webb & Sheeran 2006; Sheeran 2016, d about 0.36 to 0.66) and reappraisal changes emotion (Webb 2012). The reason-versus-emotion primacy is a standing theoretical dispute (Zajonc vs Lazarus). No independent evidence for a four-emotion taxonomy (horror, pleasure, fear, rage)

**C5a. Smoking: pleasure-driven; graphic warning images (fear) did not stop smoking**

- Label: **Not supported**
- Why: Pictorial packs raised quit attempts (40% vs 34%) and 7-day abstinence (5.7% vs 3.8%) in a 4-week RCT of 2,149 smokers (Noel Brewer et al. 2016, a different author from Judson Brewer); Canada's labels were followed by a 12 to 20 percent relative fall in smoking (Huang 2014, observational). Effects are modest and real-world results mixed. Fear works mainly with efficacy beliefs. Smoking is better framed as escape from negative affect than pleasure. **This is the claim to drop or reword**

**C5b. Phobia: knowing the plane rarely crashes does not remove the fear**

- Label: **Partly supported**
- Why: Plausible, and exposure is the best-supported phobia treatment. But cognitive therapy roughly matches exposure in several anxiety disorders (Norton 2007; Ougrin 2011), and exposure itself seems to work partly by changing expectancies (Hofmann 2008; Craske 2014). Nobody tested the flight-statistics example directly

**C6. CBT is over-represented in research, funding, guidelines and training**

- Label: **Partly supported**
- Why: Trials and funding: supported (two thirds of psychotherapy RCT arms 2010 to 2019, Rief 2022; about half of long-term depression comparisons, Cuijpers 2026; NIMH CBT share of psychotherapy dollars 44%, Galves 2025; IAPT about 68% CBT-based). Training: contested (CBT is the most-taught evidence-based therapy, but evidence-based training overall is thin, not displaced; Weissman 2006)

**C7. The bodies that decide what counts as evidence-based (guideline panels, accreditation bodies, editorial boards, funding panels) are staffed by CBT-aligned people, which crowds out other therapies**

- Label: **No evidence found** (opinion only)
- Why: I found no study of the orientation of NICE or APA panel members, editors or funding panels. The assertion comes from psychodynamic-aligned authors (Leichsenring 2018, Shedler) and is rebutted by CBT-aligned authors (David, Cristea & Hofmann 2018): both sides are interested parties. Counter-evidence: CBT's edge over other bona fide therapies is small (g = 0.06 in depression, Cuijpers 2023) and psychodynamic therapy tested equivalent (Steinert 2017), which weakens "crowding out has a cost"


**Judson Brewer.** His peer-reviewed evidence is real but narrower than the claim it is being used for: mindfulness-based craving/habit training beat a comparator in two specific trials (smoking vs the Freedom From Smoking programme, n = 88, 31% vs 6% abstinent at week 17; GAD app vs treatment as usual, n = 65), and was null in a larger smoking app trial (n = 325, 9.8% vs 12.1%). None has a CBT arm for anxiety, so none supports "CBT is dominated"; two are developer-authored. His anxiety-as-habit-loop account is a negative-reinforcement version of C3 and is theory, and the prefrontal-cortex-goes-offline claim is not tested in worry. His TED talk and book are talk and popular book, not evidence. Parity, not superiority, is what the wider literature shows for acceptance and mindfulness approaches vs CBT (A-Tjak 2015, p = .140; Khoury 2013, g = -0.07; Hoge 2023, MBSR non-inferior to escitalopram).

**Honest summary for the paper.** The strong versions of the five points mostly do not survive. The weaker versions do, and they are interesting: (1) CBT's headline effect is control-dependent, (2) allegiance is real and CBT's rivals have it too, (3) "stop trying to force it" is supported but is already inside CBT, (4) CBT dominates volume and funding, (5) whether that dominance has cost anyone is untested.

## 2. Proposed paper structure

Working title: *What the evidence for CBT can and cannot show: control conditions, allegiance, mechanism and concentration of research effort.* This fits the existing review in the repo (Draft 3, ten graded reasons R1 to R10) as new or extended reasons, rather than a separate paper.

1. **Introduction.** The question is not "does CBT work" but "how much of the measured effect is specific, and is the evidence base itself narrow." State up front that the claims are graded and some are rejected.
2. **Method.** Claim-first design: each claim is written as a testable statement with a falsifier, then searched for and against; sources are verified; verdicts per claim; labels. Pre-registered in the repo's protocol file before any further searches.
3. **C1 Control conditions and nonspecific factors.** Waitlist vs attention placebo vs active; structural equivalence; dose; disclosure/venting literature.
4. **C2 Allegiance.** Meta-research; both directions; what it implies for CBT-vs-other claims.
5. **C3 and C4 Attempted solutions.** Ironic processes, avoidance, safety behaviours, sleep effort, paradoxical intention; what Palo Alto/brief strategic therapy outcome evidence there is and is not. Include the point that CBT already targets these.
6. **C5 Cognition and emotion.** Intention-behaviour gap, reappraisal, exposure mechanisms, dual-process disputes. Use smoking warnings only as a worked example of why a plausible claim needs data (C5a goes against it).
7. **Alternatives with evidence.** Mindfulness-based habit training (Brewer's trials), ACT, MBSR, psychodynamic: parity, not dominance, with the small-trial caveats.
8. **C6 and C7 Concentration of research effort.** Descriptive counts of CBT share (trials, funding, guidelines, training) and, separately and explicitly labelled as hypothesis, the gatekeeping mechanism. Propose a study that would test it (see 3).
9. **Discussion and limits.** What would change our mind; interested-party problem on both sides; no randomised evidence on strategic therapy against CBT by independent teams.

## 3. Gaps worth filling (new data would make the paper)

- **C7 is testable and nobody has done it.** Code the stated orientation or training of guideline-panel members (NICE depression and anxiety, APA), editorial boards of the main clinical psychology journals, and funding review panels; compare with the orientation mix of the published trial base. Feasible from public rosters.
- **C6 counts.** A reproducible Europe PMC / Metapsy count of psychotherapy RCTs by orientation per decade (the repo already has a Europe PMC search baseline). Rief 2022 gives the 2010 to 2019 figure; extend and replicate.
- **C4** needs an independent RCT of strategic therapy vs CBT. Until it exists, the paper should say there is none.
- **C1** could be sharpened with a meta-regression on structural equivalence of controls within CBT trials only.

## 4. Handling and cautions

- The five starting points are an unattributed personal communication. They are not cited; the paper cites only the literature. Credit in the paper, if any, is decided with the person in conversation.
- The repo is public. This outline contains no private names and no personal health details.
- Brewer's research must be described as what the trials show; he is not a witness for "CBT is over."
- Unverified and dropped items are listed in each appendix. Notably: the MQ 2015 UK funding split and "99% of German psychology chairs are CBT" (only second-hand via Leichsenring 2018), Nardone's centre statistics, a Cochrane review of pictorial warnings (none found), and several guessed DOIs that resolved to unrelated papers.
- One correction to an earlier assumption: Weissman 2006 is in Archives of General Psychiatry, not the American Journal of Psychiatry.

#### A1 synthesis
Label: **Partly supported.** The weak form (a good share of the CBT-vs-waitlist effect is attention, expectancy and nonspecific support) is well supported. Psychological-placebo arms beat waitlist by about half an SD (Faltinsen 2022). Structurally equivalent placebos erase the specific advantage in Baskin 2003, though its abstract gives no numbers. Supportive therapy gets 49.6% "nonspecific" (Cuijpers 2012, a model-based partition). Counter-evidence: CBT still beats pill and psychological placebo modestly (Carpenter g=0.56, Hofmann OR 4.06, Bandelow, James OR 2.28 vs attention control). The specific "hour of talking" and "venting" version is weaker. Total contact time and session number did not predict effect size in depression trials (Cuijpers 2013), while disclosure-only interventions have tiny effects (r=.075, d=.19, g=-0.12), far below CBT's. So disclosure alone cannot explain the size of CBT effects, though structured supportive therapy can. Thin or opinion-only evidence: Mohr 2009 is framework, Wampold 1997 numbers are unread, and the common-factors vs specific-factors causal question is open (Cuijpers 2019).

#### A2 synthesis
Label: **Supported in general, weak for CBT specifically.** The allegiance-outcome association is robust: r about .26 across 30 meta-analyses (Munder 2013), 12% of variance even among equal-efficacy trauma therapies (Munder 2012), and a 1.31 relative odds ratio in Cochrane reviews (Dragioti 2015). Quasi-experimental pairing finds bias in about half of the pairs (Zimmerman 2024), so "ubiquitous, not inevitable" is the safe wording. Against the claim: Dragioti found no significant allegiance effect for CBT, Tolin's CBT advantage survived allegiance adjustment, and Leykin argues the association is not automatically bias. Allegiance cuts both ways: allegiance to a non-CBT therapy can raise that arm, and Cuijpers 2012 shows an allegiance-driven gap against supportive therapy that vanishes on adjustment. Allegiance bears only on head-to-head comparisons. It does not touch CBT-vs-waitlist or CBT-vs-placebo contrasts (Carpenter, Hofmann), and most head-to-head results already show CBT about equal to other bona fide therapies (Cuijpers 2023, g=0.06), so the practical size of any inflation is small. Thin evidence: no study I found estimates a CBT-specific allegiance inflation in g units, and Luborsky's 69% is an outlier.

De Mooij (children): exposure more favourable, no added benefit from restructuring. | MIXED, mostly against "reason does nothing" | already in repo

Synthesis for C2. The narrow observation is plausible: telling a phobic person the statistics rarely abolishes the fear, and exposure-based treatment is the best-supported phobia treatment (Wolitzky-Taylor 2008; Öst 1989 is uncontrolled). I found no study that directly tests "explain flight statistics" against exposure in flying phobia, so that exact example is untested here. Against the broader claim: cognitive therapy performs about as well as exposure in several anxiety disorders (Norton 2007; Ougrin 2011), reappraisal measurably reduces emotion (Webb 2012), and exposure itself seems to work by changing expectancies (Hofmann 2008; Craske 2014). So CBT is not "reason only" and the contrast is blurry.

# Appendix D. CBT dominance and self-reinforcement (C6, C7)

Claim (D): "CBT is over-represented in research, funding, guidelines and training, which is self-reinforcing because the bodies that decide what counts as evidence-based (guideline panels, accreditation/boards, journal editorial boards, funders) are staffed by CBT-aligned people, and this crowds out other therapies from being studied."

Interpretation: "psychology board" is read as guideline panels (NICE, APA), accreditation and training bodies, journal editorial boards, and funding review panels.

### Label for D: PARTLY SUPPORTED
- Part 1, over-representation of CBT in trials, publications, guidelines and psychotherapy funding: Supported.
- Part 2, over-representation in training: Contested. Surveys show CBT is the most-taught evidence-based therapy, but they do not show other therapies being displaced.
- Part 3, the mechanism (gatekeeping bodies staffed by CBT-aligned people, which crowds out other therapies): No evidence found from any empirical study. It is asserted in opinion pieces by psychodynamic-aligned authors and denied by CBT-aligned authors.

### Synthesis
- Dominance of CBT in trial volume is documented: about two thirds of RCT arms in 2010-19 (Rief 2022) and about half of long-term depression comparisons (Cuijpers 2026). CBT is the largest psychotherapy funding category at NIMH, and IAPT delivery is about 68% CBT-based by design.
- Training and guidelines are less clear-cut. Guidelines (NICE, RANZCP) list psychodynamic, IPT, counselling and others as options; US psychiatry residency requires psychodynamic competence; Weissman's survey shows evidence-based training in general is underprovided rather than CBT crowding out others.
- The mechanism is the weak link. I found no study analysing the therapy orientation of NICE or APA panel members, of editorial boards, or of funding review panels. The assertion comes from opinion papers by psychodynamic-aligned authors (Leichsenring 2018, Shedler); the rebuttal from CBT-aligned authors (David 2018) is equally interested. Allegiance bias in outcomes is real (Munder 2013, Zimmerman 2024), but that is about researchers' results, not about who sits on panels.
- Counter-evidence: comparative trials show CBT's advantage over other bona fide therapies is small (g=0.06 in depression, Cuijpers 2023) and psychodynamic therapy is equivalent in a pre-registered adversarial test (Steinert 2017). This favours the "crowding out lacks a clear cost" reading, though it also shows how few psychodynamic RCTs there are. Dominance may reflect manualisability and measurability, but that argument is mostly made informally.
- Unverified or dropped: the MQ 2015 funding split and the 99% German chairs figure (quoted secondhand); the BACP response document; any Division 12 tally; Cosgrove 2017 numbers (abstract not retrieved); the exact Metapsy-wide CBT share. Weissman 2006 is Arch Gen Psychiatry, not Am J Psychiatry as the brief says.
