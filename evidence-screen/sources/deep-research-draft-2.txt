  Reasoning Your Way Out of Anxiety? A Critical
Review of Cognitive Restructuring, Executive Function
                  and Adult ADHD
                                 Brando Miranda∗
                        Stanford University, Stanford, CA, USA




Abstract
Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is the best-studied psychotherapy for
anxiety, yet only about half of treated patients respond, and the mecha-
nism usually offered for its cognitive component, that patients change feel-
ings by examining and replacing automatic thoughts, has seldom been tested
against the cognitive demands it places on them. We ask whether cogni-
tive restructuring is an executive-function task that anxiety and attention-
deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) make harder, and whether it is the
part of CBT that carries the benefit. We specify the executive demands of
restructuring, review evidence on stress, anxiety and prefrontal function (in-
cluding a check of the popular “prefrontal cortex goes off-line” claim against
its sources), review eﬀicacy, bias and component evidence, review alternatives
and CBT in adult ADHD, and assess the sparse direct evidence on executive
function as a moderator of outcome. About 300 sources were DOI-verified.
The evidence supports a narrow claim and not a broad one. Reappraisal
draws on working memory in the laboratory, but evidence that stress abol-
ishes it is contested. CBT is effective on average, though effects shrink with
active controls and bias adjustment and have not grown over three decades.
Component studies, though underpowered, repeatedly fail to find added ben-
efit from restructuring over exposure or behavioural activation. Executive
function predicts uptake of the cognitive skill in older adults more than over-
all outcome. In adult ADHD, effective CBT protocols look behavioural, and

1. Introduction
    Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is among the most heavily tested
psychotherapies. A meta-analysis of 41 placebo-controlled trials in anxi-
ety and related disorders (total N = 2,843) found that CBT outperformed
placebo on target symptoms with a moderate effect (Hedges’ g = 0.56) (Car-
penter et al., 2018). The same literature also shows a ceiling: across 87
studies only about half of patients respond to CBT for anxiety disorders,
and the figure depends on how response is defined (Loerinc et al., 2015); a
larger meta-analysis of 100 articles reports a mean remission rate of 51.0%
(Springer et al., 2018). A therapy can work on average and still leave open
why it works and for whom.
    The textbook account of CBT’s cognitive component is a top-down loop.
A situation triggers an automatic thought (“I am going to fail this talk”);
the patient records it, names the distortion, weighs evidence for and against,
and generates a more balanced thought; the feeling is expected to follow.
This review asks a narrow question about that loop: does the mechanism ask
the patient to use a capacity that anxiety, and in some patients attention-
deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), has already degraded? If so, the cog-
nitive component should depend on baseline executive capacity, should be
harder to use when stressed than when calm, and should contribute less to
outcome than behavioural elements that do not demand effortful reasoning.
    The argument has a popular version. Judson Brewer, a clinician-researcher,
argues that anxiety takes the prefrontal cortex “off-line,” so that willpower
and reasoning are unavailable at the moments when they are needed (Brewer,
2016, 2021), and he has applied this directly to CBT (Brewer, 2026). Our
aim is to find out how much of this survives contact with the primary liter-
ature. Our answer, in brief, is that a narrow version survives and the strong
version does not.
What we find.. (1) Reappraisal does draw on working memory in laboratory
studies, but evidence that acute stress abolishes it is thin and contested.

(2) The direct evidence that executive function moderates CBT outcome is
sparse and mostly null for overall outcome, with the clearest signal at the
level of the cognitive skill itself. (3) Component studies repeatedly find that
adding cognitive restructuring to exposure or behavioural activation does not
add measurable benefit, but these studies are underpowered and a minority
find a benefit. (4) CBT’s headline effects shrink with active controls and
bias adjustment; whether they shrink over time is disputed. (5) In adult
ADHD, CBT is modestly effective, mostly as an adjunct to medication, and
the plausible active ingredient is behavioural compensation; we found no trial
testing ADHD as a moderator of CBT for anxiety in adults.
Contributions.. (i) An explicit decomposition of cognitive restructuring into
executive demands (Section 3); (ii) an evidence-graded review of the stress-
and-prefrontal premise, including a check of the “off-line” claim against its
sources (Section 4); (iii) a review of eﬀicacy, bias and component evidence
(Sections 6–7); (iv) a review of alternatives and of CBT in adult ADHD (Sec-
tions 8–9); (v) the direct moderator evidence (Section 10); (vi) preregistrable
predictions (Section 12).

2. Method
    This is a critical (theoretical) review, not a PRISMA-complete systematic
review. It selects the literatures needed to evaluate one mechanistic argument
and states what each can and cannot show. Searches ran in October 2026
across PubMed, Europe PMC, Crossref, publisher pages and the reference
lists of key meta-analyses, in four slices: (a) eﬀicacy, bias and head-to-head
comparisons; (b) mechanisms and component (dismantling) studies, includ-
ing exposure and third-wave alternatives; (c) executive function, stress and
emotion regulation, including executive function as a moderator of treatment;
and (d) ADHD (CBT, medication, mindfulness, executive function, anxiety
comorbidity). Priority was given to meta-analyses, umbrella reviews, compo-
nent trials and moderator studies, and to the strongest critiques and defences
of CBT.
Verification.. Every reference was resolved through Crossref (title, authors
and year compared with the intended citation). Every quoted number was
read from the abstract or full text, not recalled. Roughly 300 candidate
sources were verified this way; several candidate references proposed from
memory were dropped because the DOI did not resolve or resolved to a

different paper. Sources for which only a partial abstract or a secondary
summary was available are flagged in deep-research/sources/. Popular
sources (books, talks, blogs) are cited only for the claims their authors make.

Limitations of the method.. Single-reviewer screening; no pre-registered pro-
tocol; no formal risk-of-bias assessment of included reviews; evidence from
depression is used where anxiety evidence on a question is thin, and flagged
when so. A PRISMA-style umbrella search is the obvious next step (Sec-
tion 12).

3. What cognitive restructuring asks of the patient
    Consider the standard thought-record exercise. The patient must (a) no-
tice an automatic thought while it is occurring, (b) hold the thought, the
supporting evidence, the contradicting evidence and a candidate alternative
in mind at once, (c) suppress the pull of the automatic thought while evalu-
ating it, and (d) shift from being inside the feeling to observing it. Mapped
onto the best-known model of executive function, these are three separable
components: updating and holding information (working memory), inhibit-
ing a prepotent response, and shifting between mental sets (Miyake et al.,
2000).

Laboratory evidence that reappraisal draws on executive resources.. In event-
related-potential studies, high working-memory load eliminated the effect of
reappraisal on the late positive potential (Gan et al., 2017; Adamczyk et al.,
2022). Working-memory capacity predicted reappraisal success (Schmeichel
et al., 2008), and reappraising late in an emotional response, once emotion is
already intense, costs self-control resources (Sheppes and Meiran, 2008). Peo-
ple also choose differently as intensity rises: they prefer distraction to reap-
praisal in high-intensity negative situations and reappraisal in low-intensity
ones (Sheppes et al., 2011).

Evidence that it works, and can be trained, when load is low.. Instructed
reappraisal reduces negative experience in the laboratory (Hofmann et al.,
2009) and can be trained (Denny and Ochsner, 2014). So the claim is not
that reappraisal fails; it is that it costs executive resources, which matters
when those resources are scarce.




                                       4
Two clarifications.. First, this is a task analysis plus laboratory support,
not a treatment finding. Clinicians scaffold the load (worksheets, a therapist
who prompts for evidence, graded practice). Second, executive function is
not a single resource; the argument concerns working memory, inhibition and
shifting, not general intelligence. We return to both in Section 11.

4. Stress, anxiety and the prefrontal executive system
4.1. Mechanistic neuroscience
    The most developed account is due to Arnsten. Her review of stress sig-
nalling describes the prefrontal cortex (PFC) as the most evolved brain region
and also the one most sensitive to the detrimental effects of stress exposure
(Arnsten, 2009). The proposed mechanism is an inverted-U in catecholamine
signalling: moderate norepinephrine and dopamine levels support prefrontal
network firing and working memory, while the high levels of uncontrollable
stress weaken them and strengthen amygdala and striatal habit circuitry. A
later review summarises the result as stress rapidly flipping the brain from
reflective to reflexive control of behaviour (Arnsten, 2015). Acute uncontrol-
lable stress impairs PFC-dependent working memory in monkeys and rodents
(Arnsten and Goldman-Rakic, 1998). In humans, a meta-analysis finds that
acute stress impairs working memory and cognitive flexibility, with more nu-
anced effects on inhibition and many moderators (Shields et al., 2016), and
imaging suggests a reallocation of resources toward salience networks rather
than a switch-off (Hermans et al., 2014).

Where the “off-line” language comes from.. The phrase traces to a hedged
line in a 1998 monkey study (“Stress may take the PFC ‘off-line”’; Arnsten
and Goldman-Rakic 1998), and its author still uses it in quotation marks for
uncontrollable stress (Arnsten, 2020). Brewer and Roy apply it to worry and
cite Arnsten’s 2009 and 2015 reviews, which are not studies of worry (Brewer
and Roy, 2021); their app-based trial (n = 65, treatment-as-usual control)
did not test a prefrontal mechanism (Roy et al., 2021). We therefore read
“offline” as a popularisation of impaired, in a graded and dose-dependent way,
mainly under uncontrollable stress.

4.2. Does stress abolish reappraisal in humans?
    Evidence for this specific link is contested. The founding study found that
participants stressed before learning showed no fear reduction after regulation

                                      5
training (Raio et al., 2013), and a review summarises that acute stress may
impair the persistent inhibition of fear, presumably by altering prefrontal
function (Raio and Phelps, 2015). Five later studies (n = 54–98) found no
group-level impairment (Shermohammed et al., 2017; Sandner et al., 2021;
Langer et al., 2021, 2023; Hamza et al., 2024), though two reported sex- or
cortisol-linked effects. Their laboratory stressors were moderate, so the null
results may not transfer to clinical-level arousal; that gap is itself an open
question.

4.3. Cognitive psychology of anxiety
    Attentional Control Theory proposes that anxiety impairs the inhibition
and shifting functions of executive control, so that anxious individuals are
more captured by threat and worse at redirecting attention (Eysenck et al.,
2007). The theory predicts that eﬀiciency (effort and time) suffers more than
effectiveness (accuracy), because anxious people compensate with extra ef-
fort. Meta-analytic support exists at group level (g = −0.58; Shi et al., 2019)
and for working memory (g = −0.334; Moran, 2016), but with qualifiers: a
meta-analysis of 32 studies (N = 13,084) found generalised anxiety disorder
(GAD) associated with poorer cognitive flexibility and working memory but
not inhibitory control, and with no association with any executive domain in
older adults (Nguyen et al., 2025). Working memory was unrelated to social
anxiety in one study (Harrison et al., 2025), and one study found enhanced
control over negative distractors in GAD (Hallion et al., 2019).

5. Executive function in ADHD
    ADHD is heterogeneous: executive diﬀiculties are common but not uni-
versal. A meta-analysis of 83 studies found that groups with ADHD under-
perform controls on every executive task class examined, most strongly on
response inhibition, vigilance, working memory and planning, with medium
effect sizes (0.46–0.69) (Willcutt et al., 2005). The same authors caution that
executive weaknesses are neither necessary nor suﬀicient to cause all cases of
ADHD, and fewer than half of the (mostly child) samples show impairment
on any single executive test (Willcutt et al., 2005). Measured more carefully,
impairment is more prevalent: in a sample of 136 children, 89% of those
with ADHD were impaired on at least one executive function (62% working
memory, 27% inhibitory control, 38% set shifting) (Kofler et al., 2019).

     Anxiety is common in adult ADHD: a meta-analysis of 58 studies (N =
18,821) found higher anxiety scores in adults with ADHD than without
(g = 0.77; g = 0.95 against non-clinical and g = 0.44 against clinical con-
trols) (Hoskins et al., 2026). Whether anxiety adds executive load in ADHD
is less clear: in a sample of 339 children, trait anxiety did not uniquely pre-
dict working memory or inhibitory control, nor moderate the ADHD effect
(Marsh et al., 2024). The stacking assumption (low baseline executive ca-
pacity, lowered further by anxiety) is therefore plausible but, in children at
least, not supported by the one careful test we found.

6. What the eﬀicacy evidence shows
6.1. The case for CBT
    Against placebo, CBT outperforms on anxiety target symptoms (g = 0.56
across 41 trials; Carpenter et al. 2018). Against inactive controls, effects are
larger: a meta-analysis of 144 trials found g = 0.75 (depression) to 0.88
(social anxiety disorder), with 0.80 for GAD and 0.81 for panic disorder
(Cuijpers et al., 2016). A 2025 network of 375 trials gave anxiety-spectrum
effects between 0.5 and 1.0, above 1.0 for PTSD and specific phobia (Cuijpers
et al., 2025). In social anxiety, individual CBT ranked best of the therapies
compared in a network meta-analysis of 101 trials (Mayo-Wilson et al., 2014),
and in GAD only CBT remained significantly better than treatment as usual
at 3–12 months in a network of 65 trials (Papola et al., 2024). Any critique
has to explain why CBT works at all.

6.2. The headline shrinks with better controls and bias adjustment
   • Control type. More than 80% of anxiety trials used waitlist controls,
      and the few that did not showed much smaller effects (Cuijpers et al.,
      2016); only 17.4% of trials in that analysis were high quality. In the
      375-trial analysis, waitlist-controlled effects exceeded g = 0.94, while
      care-as-usual comparisons gave 0.22–1.13 (Cuijpers et al., 2025).

   • Bias. In depression, psychotherapy effects fell from g = 0.70 to
     g = 0.31 after adjusting for several sources of bias, with only 23%
     of studies at low risk of bias (Cuijpers et al., 2019a). Of NIH-funded
     psychotherapy trials, 23.6% never reached publication; including them
     reduced the pooled effect by about 25% (Driessen et al., 2015). An
     umbrella review of 102 recent meta-analyses (3,782 RCTs) found most

                                       7
      target-symptom effects small and pooled psychotherapy effects of 0.34
      against placebo or treatment as usual (Leichsenring et al., 2022).
   • Response and remission. About half of anxiety patients respond or
     remit (Loerinc et al., 2015; Springer et al., 2018).
6.3. Does the effect shrink over time?
    This is contested, and the evidence is mixed. In depression, one meta-
analysis reported a steady decline in CBT effects since its introduction (Johnsen
and Friborg, 2015), but a reanalysis restricted to randomised comparisons
judged the decline “most likely a spurious finding” (Cristea et al., 2017), and
a 409-trial meta-analysis found no reduction by publication year (Cuijpers
et al., 2023). In anxiety, a meta-regression of 49 controlled studies over 30
years found a flat trend (publication year B = −0.008, p = .24): effects
“did not increase over the last 3 decades” (Hofmann et al., 2025). Placebo-
controlled anxiety estimates have been reported at lower values in successive
reviews (0.73, 0.56, 0.24), but those come from separate reviews with differ-
ent case mixes, not from one meta-regression. We therefore do not claim a
decline over time; the defensible statement is that effects have not grown and
that they are sensitive to control condition and bias.

7. Where the active ingredients may lie
7.1. Component and dismantling evidence
    The foundational dismantling trial (depression, N = 150) found no evi-
dence that the complete cognitive treatment outperformed behavioural acti-
vation alone or behavioural activation plus automatic-thought skills at ter-
mination or at 6 months (Jacobson et al., 1996), and a large non-inferiority
trial found behavioural activation as effective as CBT (mean PHQ-9 differ-
ence 0.1; Richards et al. 2016). In anxiety:
   • In a 108-trial meta-analysis, cognitive therapy, exposure and their com-
     binations were eﬀicacious with “no differential eﬀicacy for any treat-
     ment components” (Norton and Price, 2007).
   • In a full-factorial trial of internet CBT for social anxiety (N = 464),
     conditions with psychoeducation and exposure were more effective,
     while conditions with cognitive restructuring or attention training were
     not superior at post-treatment; the advantage of exposure faded at
     follow-up (Šipka et al., 2025).

                                      8
   • Adding cognitive restructuring to prolonged exposure did not enhance
     outcome in assault survivors with PTSD (Foa et al., 2005), nor in
     children with social anxiety (de Mooij et al., 2023).

   • A component network meta-analysis in panic disorder (72 studies) sin-
     gled out interoceptive exposure, not cognitive restructuring, as eﬀicacy-
     enhancing (Pompoli et al., 2018).

   • In GAD, the network meta-analysis authors concluded that pure cog-
     nitive restructuring was not a decisively active ingredient when con-
     sidered alone (Papola et al., 2024); the paper was later corrected for
     a duplicated cohort and an error in standard-error handling (JAMA
     Psychiatry, 2024).

7.2. Evidence against the claim
    These findings are not uniform.

   • Cognitive therapy beat exposure in social phobia in a meta-analysis of
     20 direct comparisons (N = 1,308), with no difference in PTSD, OCD
     or panic (Ougrin, 2011).

   • In a trauma trial, adding cognitive restructuring to exposure was asso-
     ciated with PTSD rates of 31% versus 63–75% without it at 6 months
     (Bryant et al., 2008).

   • In-session cognitive restructuring correlates with outcome (r = .35),
     though from only four studies and correlationally (Ezawa and Hollon,
     2023).

   • In a single-session trial (N = 249), restructuring before exposure worked
     about as well as behavioural experiments and better than exposure
     without explicit processing (Johnco et al., 2025).

   • The exposure–cognition split is blurred: exposure may itself work by
     changing the expectancy of harm (Hofmann, 2008).

7.3. Why the component evidence is weak on both sides
    A review of 16 component studies concluded that they lack “the statistical
power nor the quality to draw any meaningful conclusion,” because none
except one could detect an effect smaller than g = 0.55 (Cuijpers et al., 2017).

                                       9
Mediation evidence is correlational: cognitive change tracks symptom change
however it is produced (Cristea et al., 2015; Lorenzo-Luaces et al., 2015),
cognitive mediation of symptom reduction was limited in a depression cohort
(median r = .06; Vittengl et al., 2014), and reviewers say no common or
specific factor meets causal criteria (Kazdin, 2007; Cuijpers et al., 2019b). A
null component result is thus weak evidence that restructuring adds nothing,
and a positive correlation is weak evidence that it matters.

7.4. The strongest defence: practice when calm
    A defender can answer that patients do not restructure during a panic;
they practise when calm so the response becomes automatic. We accept the
force of this. It changes the claim, though: if rehearsal during calm periods
does the work, the active ingredient is practice and habit formation, and the
relevant question is the executive cost of each rehearsal. Exposure, which is
central to anxiety CBT, is behavioural, and optimised exposure protocols are
explicitly framed around expectancy violation and extinction (Craske et al.,
2014).

8. Alternatives and their evidence
8.1. Mindfulness and acceptance-based treatments
    The alternative that follows from the argument is to train attention and
acceptance directly rather than dispute thoughts. Brewer frames anxiety as
a habit loop (trigger, worry, a feeling of control) and proposes noticing, cu-
riosity about the reward value of worry, and a “bigger, better offer” (Brewer,
2021). Evidence for the specific programme is early: in a randomised trial of
an app-based programme for GAD (n = 65), anxiety scores fell 67% versus
14% under usual care, with mediation implicating reduced worry (Roy et al.,
2021). The trial is small, self-reported, compared with usual care rather than
CBT, and has the programme’s developer among its authors.
    Against evidence-based comparators, mindfulness and acceptance ap-
proaches show parity, not superiority. A meta-analysis found essentially no
difference (g ≈ 0.00) between mindfulness-based interventions and evidence-
based treatments, often CBT (Goldberg et al., 2018); another found no differ-
ence from traditional CBT or behavioural therapies (g = −.07, nine compar-
isons; Khoury et al. 2013). For acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT)
versus CBT, the pooled difference was non-significant in one meta-analysis
(A-Tjak et al., 2014), and a sceptical reading of 60 RCTs concluded that

                                      10
ACT is not yet well-established for any disorder (Öst, 2014). In 276 adults
with anxiety disorders, eight weeks of mindfulness-based stress reduction was
noninferior to escitalopram, with fewer discontinuations for adverse events
(Hoge et al., 2023). That is parity with a first-line drug, not superiority to
CBT. The convergence of camps is itself informative: if CBT, behavioural
activation, ACT and mindfulness are roughly equivalent, either common fac-
tors dominate or several mechanisms lead to the same place.

8.2. Medication in ADHD
     In a network meta-analysis of 133 double-blind trials, amphetamines
(standardised effect −0.79) and methylphenidate (−0.49) outperformed placebo
in adults on clinician ratings, over short follow-up (Cortese et al., 2018). The
certainty varied from high or moderate to low or very low. A Cochrane re-
view of extended-release methylphenidate found a self-rated effect of −0.37,
judged 20 of 21 trials at high risk of bias, and noted that all trials excluded
participants with psychiatric comorbidity such as anxiety or selected previ-
ous responders (Boesen et al., 2022). So the pivotal medication trials say
little about adults with ADHD and anxiety.

9. CBT in adult ADHD
9.1. Eﬀicacy
    A randomised trial of 86 medication-treated adults found that CBT out-
performed relaxation with educational support, with 53% versus 23% re-
sponders on clinician global ratings (Safren et al., 2010); a smaller earlier
trial found 56% versus 13% responders for CBT added to medication (Safren
et al., 2005). A meta-analysis of 32 studies found a between-group effect of
g = .65 on symptoms, smaller with active controls, and neither medication
status nor treatment length moderated outcome (Knouse et al., 2017). An-
other found SMD 0.76 against waitlist but 0.43 against active controls (Young
et al., 2020), and a 2026 meta-analysis of 14 RCTs found SMD −0.45 on core
symptoms and −0.43 on executive function (Liu et al., 2026). A component
network meta-analysis of 113 RCTs found CBT better than placebo only on
clinician-reported measures (Ostinelli et al., 2025), and blinded-rater effects
of psychological treatments were attenuated to non-significance in an earlier
synthesis that did not separate adult from child samples (Sonuga-Barke et al.,
2013). An umbrella review judged CBT, atomoxetine and methylphenidate
to have at least moderate-certainty evidence in adults, with no high-certainty

                                      11
long-term evidence for any intervention (Gosling et al., 2025). The Cochrane
review rates the CBT evidence as low quality (Lopez et al., 2018).

9.2. What the protocols contain, and where CBT sits
    The effective protocols are skills-heavy: time management, organisation
and planning, with adaptive thinking as one module (Safren et al., 2010;
Solanto et al., 2010). One review argued that learning behavioural compen-
satory strategies “may be a critical active ingredient” (Knouse and Safren,
2010), and the authors of one trial proposed behavioural control as the key
mechanism (Young et al., 2017). We found no dismantling study of cognitive
restructuring in adult ADHD, so this is inference. In the largest multi-centre
trial (N = 433), group CBT did not beat structured clinical management,
while methylphenidate beat placebo (Philipsen et al., 2015). Guidelines posi-
tion CBT as an adjunct to medication (Kooij et al., 2019; National Institute
for Health and Care Excellence, 2018). Results on adding medication to CBT
are mixed: no incremental benefit in a small double-blind pilot (Weiss et al.,
2012), but greater early improvement in an unblinded trial (N = 88), with
the gap narrowing over follow-up (Cherkasova et al., 2020).

9.3. Anxiety in ADHD
    In a meta-analysis of 13 RCTs (n = 744), CBT-based interventions re-
duced anxiety in ADHD modestly (SMD −0.29 post-treatment, −0.42 at
follow-up), with effects more apparent in adults; most trials delivered ADHD-
focused rather than anxiety-targeted CBT (Wang et al., 2026). Another
meta-analysis found CBT improved anxiety, particularly at follow-up, pre-
dicted by core-symptom reduction (López-Pinar et al., 2020). In children
with primary anxiety in group CBT (N = 842), neither ADHD diagnosis nor
subtype predicted response or remission, which cuts against the strong form
of our thesis (Gould et al., 2018). The adult version of that question appears
untested.

10. Does executive function moderate CBT outcome?
    This is the question on which the argument stands or falls. The direct
evidence is thin and mostly from older adults.

  • Supports (at the level of the skill). In older adults, poorer cogni-
    tive flexibility predicted poorer cognitive-restructuring quality and less

                                     12
      distress reduction from restructuring, but not overall CBT outcome:
      those with poorer flexibility “can still benefit from standardised CBT”
      (Johnco et al., 2014).
   • Supports (pilot). In a pilot of CBT for GAD in older adults, par-
     ticipants whose executive scores remained low did not respond, while
     those whose scores improved responded as well as an intact-executive
     group (Mohlman and Gorman, 2005).
   • Null or mixed. No baseline executive effect was found in adult OCD
     (Vandborg et al., 2016), child anxiety (Godovich et al., 2020) or brief
     psychotherapy for late-life depression (Schurr et al., 2025); a system-
     atic review of late-life anxiety found no replicated executive predictor
     (Kyriacou et al., 2023). In a meta-analysis of antidepressant response
     in late-life depression, only planning and organisation predicted out-
     come (Pimontel et al., 2016).
   • Opposite direction. Better executive performance went with non-
     response to exposure-based CBT in paediatric OCD (Hybel et al.,
     2017), and worse set-shifting went with better CBT response in late-life
     depression (Goodkind et al., 2016).
   • Adaptation helps. Problem-solving therapy beat supportive therapy
     in older adults with executive impairment, with a larger advantage
     at greater impairment (Areán et al., 2010), which points to adapting
     therapy rather than abandoning it.
Taken together, “low executive function means CBT fails” is not supported.
A narrower claim is: executive capacity may limit uptake of the cognitive skill
without limiting overall response, because CBT also works through routes
that do not need it. We found no adequately powered study of working-age
adults with ADHD and anxiety.

11. The strongest objections
   1. CBT has a large evidence base. Yes (Section 6). The argument
      concerns mechanism and moderators, not average eﬀicacy.
   2. CBT for adult ADHD works. It beats matched controls in medi-
      cated adults with a skills-heavy protocol; the evidence is graded low to
      moderate (Section 9).

                                      13
  3. Patients practise when calm. Accepted (Section 7); it relocates the
     active ingredient to practice.
  4. Exposure is behavioural. Likewise; it strengthens the argument
     against restructuring specifically, though exposure may act via changed
     expectancies (Hofmann, 2008).
  5. Mindfulness is not better. True. The argument needs only that
     restructuring is not uniquely necessary.
  6. Cognitive therapy sometimes wins. Yes, in social phobia (Ougrin,
     2011) and in one PTSD trial (Bryant et al., 2008).
  7. The neuroscience is about acute stress. Correct, and the human
     reappraisal-under-stress findings are mixed (Section 4).
  8. Executive function does not predict CBT outcome. Largely
     true for overall outcome (Section 10); the argument survives only in
     the narrower, skill-level form.
  9. Therapists scaffold the executive load. Plausible and untested;
     a therapist who generates the evidence list lowers demand and could
     erase a moderator effect.

Limitations. Narrative review by a non-clinician; single-reviewer screening;
several key studies are in older adults or children; evidence from depression
stands in for anxiety on some questions (component trials, bias adjustment,
decline over time).

12. Predictions and a research agenda
   The argument yields predictions that could fail.
  1. P1 (skill-level moderation). Baseline executive function predicts
     acquisition of restructuring skill more than acquisition of exposure-
     based skills, and predicts overall outcome only weakly.
  2. P2 (stress-dependence). The gap between calm and in-the-moment
     reappraisal is larger in low-executive-function samples and at higher
     arousal than the moderate stressors used so far.
  3. P3 (component). In adults with ADHD and anxiety, an exposure-
     and-behaviour protocol is not inferior to the same protocol plus re-
     structuring.
  4. P4 (ADHD). ADHD status predicts less benefit from the cognitive
     module specifically.

What would refute the argument: an adequately powered study find-
ing no moderation of skill acquisition by executive function; a component
trial showing that restructuring adds substantial benefit over exposure; or
meta-regression showing executive-function-heavy samples respond as well.
The cheapest next steps are (i) an individual-participant or meta-regression
analysis of existing trials (see experiments/), (ii) a PRISMA-style umbrella
search to lift this review’s method to the target journal’s standard, and (iii)
a power calculation for a moderator trial.

13. Conclusion
    Restructuring-heavy CBT is effective on average, though less so against
active controls and after bias adjustment, and its effects have not grown over
three decades. What is not established is that the cognitive component is the
active ingredient, or that it fails for people with low executive function. The
best-supported claims are narrow: reappraisal draws on working memory;
component studies, though underpowered, repeatedly fail to find an added
benefit of restructuring over behavioural elements; executive capacity may
limit the uptake of the cognitive skill but not overall response; and in adult
ADHD the effective ingredients look behavioural. The strong, popular form
of the argument, that anxiety takes the prefrontal cortex “off-line,” is not
supported by the primary literature. Direct tests in adults with ADHD and
anxiety are the missing evidence.
