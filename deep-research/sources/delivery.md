# SLICE C: therapist, delivery and real-world factors (CBT failure review)

Bib: /var/lib/agent/scratch/at824/C.bib (39 entries, all Crossref OK; authors truncated to 6 + others where >20). One extra source is already in repo (kazantzis2016quality) and is cited below without re-adding.

## Synthesis (10 lines)
1. Therapists matter, but modestly: pooled therapist effects are about 5% of outcome variance (range 0.2-29%), larger in primary care and with more severe patients, and heterogeneous by setting (johns2019therapist, firth2015pwp, saxon2012therapist). Individual therapist recovery rates in one large routine dataset ranged 23.5%-95.6%.
2. Adherence and competence are NOT reliably linked to outcome in the meta-analysis (webb2010adherence: r = .02 and .07, both n.s., heterogeneous, modalities pooled), but single studies of CBT competence find effects, mostly for specific subgroups or deterioration (kuyken2008competence, strunk2010competence, branson2015competence). Honest reading: the mechanism of "poor delivery causes failure" is plausible but not established.
3. Alliance-outcome r about .28 across 295 studies, equal for internet-delivered therapy (r about .275); this is correlational and not CBT-specific (fluckiger2018alliance); therapist-level (not patient-level) alliance variation predicts outcome (baldwin2007alliance).
4. Homework compliance is associated with outcome (r = .26, 23 studies; g about .79 post-treatment for quantity), but this is correlational (patients who improve do more homework) (mausbach2010homework, kazantzis2016quality).
5. Dose: improvement is curvilinear; most responders in routine care respond within 7 (low-intensity) or 14 (high-intensity) sessions; weekly sessions beat sparser schedules; session number alone is weakly related to effect size in trials (robinson2020dose, cuijpers2013sessions); intensified exposure scheduling was equivalent in outcome with faster response and lower dropout (pittig2021intensified).
6. Therapist drift and training gap: clinicians under-use core behavioural/exposure components, and training that lacks supervision/active learning does not change competence (waller2009drift, waller2016driftredux, becker2004exposure, shafran2009gap, beidas2010training, rakovshik2016supervision). Experience alone does not improve outcomes (goldberg2016experience).
7. FAIR TO CBT: CBT transfers to routine care. Effectiveness-study effect sizes are large and close to efficacy benchmarks for anxiety (stewart2009effectiveness) and somewhat lower for depression (hans2013effectiveness); clinically representative conditions do not erase effects (shadish2000representative); IAPT shows large pre-post effects (d = 0.87 depression, 0.88 anxiety; wakefield2021iapt) but roughly 40% reliable recovery in year one (gyani2013recovery); service organisation features predicted IAPT outcomes and between-year changes in them (clark2018iapt). Independent diagnostic follow-up of a small IAPT sample gave a much lower recovery figure (scott2018iapt; n = 90, single author, treat as critique not estimate). Youth EBPs beat usual care only modestly (weisz2013youthusual).
8. Digital delivery: guided iCBT is equivalent to face-to-face CBT in head-to-head trials (carlbring2018icbt), guided beats unguided by a small margin at post-treatment only (karyotaki2021icbtnma), and unguided is effective vs control (karyotaki2017selfguided) and works in routine care (etzelmueller2020routine). But a large pragmatic trial of supported cCBT in primary care found no benefit over usual care with low uptake (gilbody2015reeact), and e-therapy has the highest dropout among CBT formats (fernandez2015dropout).
9. Group vs individual: equivalent when treatments are identical (burlingame2016group), so format is not a major failure driver. One-size-fits-all: manualisation has no direct empirical superiority over non-manualised treatment (truijens2019manual); stratified (algorithm-matched) care modestly beat stepped care (delgadillo2022stratified, OR 1.40), stepped care is at least as good as usual care but unproven as superior (firth2015stepped), and outcome-feedback helps patients not on track (delgadillo2018feedback).
10. Overall: delivery-side evidence supports "CBT works in routine care but with lower and more variable yield, 15-26% dropout, and service/therapist/dose factors that are modifiable", rather than "CBT fails in the real world". Key gaps: causal evidence on competence, drift prevalence numbers, and trials of training/supervision on patient outcomes.
