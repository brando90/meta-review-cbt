# SLICE B: Patient and context factors that limit CBT (35 sources; all DOIs resolved OK via Crossref; BibTeX in B.bib)

SYNTHESIS
1. Comorbid personality disorder roughly doubles the odds of poor depression outcome regardless of treatment modality (OR 2.16), so the effect is a prognostic factor for all treatments and is not CBT-specific; likewise childhood maltreatment predicts non-response (OR 1.43) in depression trials.
2. For anxiety disorders, overall diagnostic comorbidity is generally unrelated to treatment effect size (Olatunji 2010), so "comorbidity kills CBT" is overstated; the effect is diagnosis-specific.
3. Baseline severity does not moderate CBT vs antidepressant differences (Weitz IPD, 1700 patients), and CBT also helps medication-refractory depression long term (CoBalT). Chronicity and trauma-focused PTSD are the harder cases: ~2/3 of veterans in CPT/PE trials still met PTSD criteria after treatment; chronic depression effect d=0.23.
4. Autistic adults: the RCT base is thin and low certainty (10 RCTs, n=537, very uncertain for most outcomes); earlier meta-analysis found effects depend on rater (non-significant on self-report g=0.24, g=0.66-0.73 on informant/clinician). Absence of evidence, not demonstrated failure.
5. Intellectual disability and dementia/MCI: CBT-type therapies produce small effects (g=0.43 in ID with weak methods; SMD -0.23 for depression in dementia, uncertain for anxiety); methodological weakness limits conclusions.
6. Age is not a barrier: older adults do better in routine IAPT data (reliable recovery OR 1.33, attrition OR 0.48), and CBT remission for late-life anxiety was 54%. Medical comorbidity attenuates effects (CBT-I effects larger for psychiatric than medical comorbidity; CBT for chronic pain only very small to small effects, SMD -0.09 to -0.34).
7. Culture and representation: culturally adapted interventions beat other conditions (g=0.67) and unadapted versions (g=0.52 Hall; d=0.32 Benish), but trials are WEIRD-skewed, exclude linguistic minorities, and almost never report effects by ethnicity; adaptation effects are heterogeneous, with trial-level quality problems.
8. Access and context: deprivation, waiting times and missed appointments predict worse outcomes in IAPT (service organisation features explained a third of variance in reliable improvement); dropout from CBT is 15.9% pre-treatment plus 26.2% during treatment, highest in depression.
9. LMIC and task-sharing: lay-worker delivered CBT-derived/behavioural therapies work (Friendship Bench adjusted mean difference -4.86 SSQ-14; HAP BDI-II diff -7.57; Pakistan trial OR 0.22 for major depression), with moderate pooled effects (SMD ~0.5), so CBT "fails" more through access than through efficacy.
10. Process view: rumination/worry are both predictors and targets; rumination-focused CBT helped residual depression (small phase II RCT), and targeting repetitive negative thinking cut 12-month depression/GAD prevalence in a prevention trial. Substance use: CBT beats minimal treatment but not other specific therapies (no CBT-specific advantage).
