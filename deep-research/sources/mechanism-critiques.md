# Slice D: theory and mechanism critiques (40 sources, all DOIs resolved via Crossref, BibTeX in D.bib)

SYNTHESIS
1. The mechanism claim is weaker than the efficacy claim. Kazdin (2009) states there is "no evidence-based explanation of how or why even the most well-studied interventions produce change"; Kraemer 2002 and Kazdin set the bar (temporal precedence, specificity, dose-response, experiment) that most CBT mediation studies do not meet.
2. Positive mediation evidence exists but is mixed. Sudden gains in CBT were preceded by cognitive change in two datasets (Tang 1999, 2005); Quilty 2008 supports the cognitive mediational model for CBT vs IPT, but the reverse "complication" model (symptom change drives attitude change) fits CBT vs pharmacotherapy. Driessen & Hollon 2010 call the mediation evidence "not yet conclusive"; Hundt 2013 finds skill use preliminarily supported but methodologically limited.
3. Cognitive-model foundations are modest: cognitive vulnerability-stress interactions predict later depression only weakly in youth (Lakdawalla 2007), and cognitive restructuring alone, behavioural activation and full CBT show no differences in efficacy (Ciharova 2021), which fits a "non-specific/behavioural change" reading as well as a "cognitive change" one. Beck & Haigh 2014 and Hofmann 2013 are the CBT-side defences.
4. Alternative mechanisms: inhibitory learning (Vervliet 2013, Weisman 2018, Pittig 2021) reframes exposure as expectancy-violation, not belief change; thought suppression and experiential avoidance (Wenzlaff 2000, Hayes 1996) give the third-wave counter-theory, but the AAQ-II may largely measure distress (Wolgast 2014), and third-wave trials were less stringent (Ost 2008).
5. Common factors/expectancy are real and replicable but small-to-moderate: alliance r = .278 (Fluckiger 2018), expectancy r = .18 (Constantino 2018), therapist effects about 5% of outcome variance (Johns 2019). Wampold 2015 argues they outweigh specific ingredients; Enck & Zipfel 2019 frame placebo/context effects in psychotherapy. This is contested, not settled, and does not show CBT "fails".
6. Measurement problem: clinician-rated scales show larger effects than self-report (delta g = 0.20; Cuijpers 2010), depression scales share little symptom content (mean Jaccard 0.36; Fried 2017) and lack unidimensionality/longitudinal invariance (Fried 2016), so "response" and "change" are partly instrument-dependent; Lambert & Ogles 2009 argue for a common clinical-significance procedure.
7. Evidence quality: only 11 of 115 depression trials met 8 quality criteria and showed d = 0.22 vs 0.74 (Cuijpers 2009/2010); excess significant findings, average power 49% (Flint 2014); few trials prospectively registered (Cybulski 2016); selective reporting inflates SMD (Miguel 2021); a Cochrane-method review of CT vs TAU found all 8 trials at high risk of bias (Jakobsen 2011).
8. Fairness: a multiverse analysis of 4281 meta-analyses still gave mean g = 0.56 with 90% clinically relevant (Plessen 2023), so bias shrinks but does not erase psychotherapy effects.
9. Future-of-field arguments: process-based therapy (Hofmann & Hayes 2018; Hayes & Hofmann 2021) is itself a critique of syndrome-specific protocols; Cuijpers 2015/2019 argue for mechanism research, patient-defined and standardised outcomes, and less redundant trials.
10. Caveat for the review: nothing in this slice demonstrates a mechanism by which CBT specifically fails; it shows the theory-of-change is under-tested, the outcome measures are heterogeneous, and the trial base is biased upward. Treat these as limits on certainty, not as evidence of ineffectiveness.
