# SLICE A: non-response, dropout, relapse/durability, deterioration and negative effects of CBT (anxiety and depression, adults)

Synthesis (all numbers below are quoted from abstracts or full text read; see per-source lines):
1. Dropout from CBT is real but not unusually high: about 1 in 5 across all psychotherapy (19.7%, Swift 2012), 26.2% during treatment plus 15.9% pre-treatment in CBT specifically (Fernandez 2015), 17.5% in depression RCTs (Cooper 2015) and about 25% in routine-care CBT for depression (Hans 2013). Depression has the highest CBT attrition; e-therapy the highest by format; therapy orientation does not moderate dropout.
2. CBT dropout is, if anything, lower than pharmacotherapy dropout (Cuijpers 2013) and not different from other psychotherapies; predictors are weak alliance (d = .55, Sharf 2010), comorbid personality disorder and minority status (Cooper 2015), male sex, low education and comorbid anxiety in self-guided online CBT (Karyotaki 2015).
3. Non-response is substantial even under trial conditions: in a moderate-to-severe depression RCT 16-week response was 58% and remission 40% for cognitive therapy (DeRubeis 2005); across 92 psychotherapy studies fewer than half met response/remission criteria, and 43-48% of control/care-as-usual patients also no longer met MDD criteria, so the added value over usual care was about 14 percentage points (Cuijpers 2014). In anxiety a review estimates about a fifth drop out and a third of completers do not respond (Taylor 2012).
4. Real-world service data show lower recovery than trials: in 19,395 IAPT patients 40.3% reliably recovered, 63.7% reliably improved and 6.6% reliably deteriorated (Gyani 2013); effectiveness-study effect sizes for depression CBT are smaller than benchmark RCTs (Hans 2013), whereas for anxiety they are within the range of efficacy trials (Stewart 2009). Service-level organisational factors explain a sizeable share of outcome variance (Clark 2018).
5. Predictors of poor outcome that have some support: comorbid personality disorder (OR 2.18 for poor depression outcome, Newton-Howes 2006), chronicity (psychotherapy effect on chronic depression small, d = 0.23, and weaker than pharmacotherapy, Cuijpers 2010), severe comorbidity and high expressed emotion in anxiety (Taylor 2012, with the caveat that "no predictor has been consistently supported"). Baseline severity did NOT moderate CBT vs medication outcome in an IPD meta-analysis of 1700 patients (Weitz 2015).
6. Durability: CBT has an enduring effect relative to stopping medication (relapse OR 2.61 favouring CBT vs medication discontinuation; no sig. difference vs continued medication; Cuijpers 2013; Hollon 2005 30.8% vs 76.2%), but many responders still relapse: 29% within 1 year and 54% within 2 years after CT discontinuation (Vittengl 2007). Continuation-phase CT lowers relapse in high-risk patients (Jarrett 2001). In anxiety, effects persist to 12+ months for GAD, social anxiety and PTSD but were not significant for panic disorder at 12+ months, and relapse rates were reported in only 6 RCTs (van Dis 2020).
7. Treatment-resistant depression: adjunctive CBT beats usual care alone (response 46% vs 22%, Wiles 2013; benefit still present at 3-5 years but mean BDI-II was still 19.2 in the CBT arm, Wiles 2016); the Cochrane review rates the evidence moderate, with most long-term evidence from one trial (Ijaz 2018).
8. Deterioration: in guided/Internet CBT trials deterioration is LOWER than in controls (5.8% vs 17.4%, Rozental 2017; 3.36% vs 7.60%, Ebert 2016), but low-education patients show higher risk and do not differ from controls (Ebert 2016). In routine care 6.6% reliably deteriorate (Gyani 2013).
9. Negative effects beyond symptom worsening are common and under-recorded: 5.2% of 14,587 UK psychological-therapy patients reported lasting bad effects (Crawford 2016); unwanted events are expected in about 5-20% of patients (Linden 2014); in 100 CBT cases therapists reported side effects in 43 patients (Schermuly-Haupt 2018); an RCT review finds adverse events likely in more than 1 in 10 participants and serious AEs in more than 1 in 21 (Klatte 2023), yet only 21% of 132 psychological-intervention RCTs monitored harms (Jonsson 2014) and none of 82 NIHR psychological trials mentioned an AE in the final report (Duggan 2014).
10. Mitigation with evidence: routine outcome monitoring/feedback reduces deterioration and about doubles reliable change in patients predicted to do poorly (Lambert 2018) and improved outcomes for not-on-track patients in an RCT of 2233 patients (Delgadillo 2018, d = 0.23 PHQ-9). Fair-to-CBT points: CBT is comparable to medication acutely (Weitz 2015, DeRubeis 2005), better tolerated than a switch to another antidepressant (Thase 2007), and effective in primary-care anxiety (CALM 63.7% vs 44.7% response, Roy-Byrne 2010).

## Dropout and attrition
- Fernandez2015Dropout | Fernandez 2015 | meta-analysis of 115 studies, 20,995 participants receiving CBT | weighted dropout 15.9% pre-treatment and 26.2% during treatment; highest in depression; e-therapy highest by format; fewer inpatient than outpatient dropouts; not associated with RCT vs non-RCT design | VERIFIED (abstract) | headline CBT dropout and moderators
- Swift2012Premature | Swift 2012 | meta-analysis of 669 studies, 83,834 clients (all adult psychotherapy) | weighted dropout 19.7% (95% CI 18.7-20.7); moderated by diagnosis, age, provider experience, setting, definition; NOT moderated by therapy orientation or individual vs group format; higher for younger clients, personality/eating disorders and trainee clinicians | VERIFIED (abstract) | benchmark dropout across psychotherapy; CBT not special
- Cooper2015DropoutMDD | Cooper 2015 | random-effects meta-analysis of 54 RCTs, N=5852, individual psychotherapy for unipolar depression | weighted dropout 19.9% study level, 17.5% psychotherapy conditions; orientation not significant; higher with more minority-status patients or comorbid personality disorder | VERIFIED (abstract) | dropout in depression RCTs and its predictors
- Hans2013Depression | Hans 2013 | meta-analysis of 34 nonrandomised effectiveness studies of outpatient CBT for adult depression | ITT d = 1.06 (completer 1.13); weighted mean dropout 24.63%; gains maintained at 6 months; effect sizes inferior to benchmark RCTs; "considerable dropout, especially in individual CBT" | VERIFIED (abstract) | routine-care effectiveness gap and dropout; also evidence CBT works in practice
- Stewart2009Anxiety | Stewart 2009 | meta-analysis of 56 effectiveness studies of CBT for adult anxiety disorders | large pre-post effects on disorder-specific measures; results within range of selected efficacy trials; small inverse relationship between clinical representativeness and outcome | VERIFIED (abstract) | fair-to-CBT: transports to routine anxiety care
- Sharf2010Alliance | Sharf 2010 | meta-analysis of 11 studies, n=1301, adult individual psychotherapy | alliance-dropout relationship d = .55; weaker alliance predicts dropout; moderated by education, treatment length, setting | VERIFIED (abstract) | predictor of dropout (not CBT-specific)
- Karyotaki2015Dropout | Karyotaki 2015 | IPD meta-analysis, 2705 participants, 10 RCTs of self-guided web-based interventions for depression | male gender RR 1.08, primary-education-only RR 1.26, comorbid anxiety RR 1.18 raised dropout; each additional 4 years of age RR 0.94 | VERIFIED (abstract) | who drops out of unguided online CBT-type programmes
- VanBallegooijen2014Adherence | van Ballegooijen 2014 | meta-analysis of 24 studies (26 conditions; 14 face-to-face CBT, 12 guided iCBT) for adult depression | face-to-face CBT participants completed 83.9% of treatment vs 80.8% guided iCBT (n.s.); completers 84.7% face-to-face vs 65.1% guided iCBT; no head-to-head trials met criteria | VERIFIED (abstract) | adherence differs by delivery format
- Taylor2012NonResponse | Taylor 2012 | narrative review of meta-analyses plus predictor review, anxiety disorders (CBT and SRIs) | "about a fifth of patients drop out prematurely and a third of treatment completers are classified as non-responders"; few reliable predictors; suggestive: low motivation, side effects, practical barriers (dropout); severe pretreatment psychopathology, comorbidity, high expressed emotion (poor response) | VERIFIED (abstract) | anxiety non-adherence/non-response overview and weak predictors

## Response, remission, effectiveness in routine care
- DeRubeis2005CT | DeRubeis 2005 | RCT, n=240 moderate-severe MDD (CT n=60, paroxetine n=120, placebo n=60) | week-8 response 43% CT vs 50% medication vs 25% placebo; week-16 response 58% in each active arm; remission 46% medication, 40% CT; CT inferior to medication at one site, linked to therapist experience | VERIFIED (abstract) | benchmark non-remission rate and therapist-expertise dependency; CBT as effective as medication under expert delivery
- Cuijpers2014Remission | Cuijpers 2014 | meta-analysis of 92 studies, 181 conditions, 6937 patients with MDD | after psychotherapy 62% no longer met MDD criteria vs 43% in controls and 48% in care-as-usual (added value about 14%); less than half met response/remission criteria after psychotherapy | VERIFIED (abstract) | absolute non-response/remission rates and large control-group improvement
- Weitz2015Severity | Weitz 2015 | IPD meta-analysis, 16 of 24 trials, n=1700 (794 CBT, 906 antidepressants) | small main effect of medication on HAM-D (beta -0.88, P=.03) but no significant difference in response (OR 1.24, P=.12) or remission (OR 1.18, P=.22); baseline severity did not moderate CBT vs medication outcomes | VERIFIED (abstract) | CBT roughly equals medication; severity not a differential predictor
- Cuijpers2010Chronic | Cuijpers 2010 | meta-analysis of 16 RCTs, psychotherapy for chronic depression and dysthymia | psychotherapy vs control d = 0.23; vs pharmacotherapy d = -0.31 (attributed to dysthymia studies); combined better than medication alone (d = 0.23); no dropout difference; at least 18 sessions suggested | VERIFIED (abstract) | chronicity as a non-response context; dose
- Newton2006PD | Newton-Howes 2006 | meta-analysis of published studies, depression with and without personality disorder | comorbid personality disorder associated with doubling of poor-outcome risk (OR 2.18, 95% CI 1.70-2.80); all treatments except ECT showed poor outcome | VERIFIED (abstract) | predictor of non-response (all treatments, not CBT-specific)
- RoyByrne2010CALM | Roy-Byrne 2010 | RCT, n=1004 primary-care anxiety patients, CALM (CBT, medication or both, outcome monitoring) vs usual care, 17 clinics | 12-month response 63.66% vs 44.68%, remission 51.49% vs 33.28%; NNT 5.27 (response), 5.50 (remission) | VERIFIED (abstract) | CBT-based collaborative care works in primary care yet about 36% non-response in intervention arm at 12 months
- Gyani2013IAPT | Gyani 2013 | routine cohort, 19,395 IAPT patients (year one, 32 services) | 40.3% reliably recovered, 63.7% reliably improved, 6.6% reliably deteriorated; non-NICE-recommended treatments had lower recovery; higher recovery with more sessions, step-up, larger services, more experienced staff | VERIFIED (abstract) | real-world recovery and deterioration rates; service-level predictors
- Clark2018IAPT | Clark 2018 | analysis of public IAPT data 2014-15 and 2015-16 | five organisational features predicted outcome (more sessions, higher % referrals treated, % with problem descriptor positive; longer waits and % missed appointments negative); moving from lowest to highest level could improve outcomes by 11-42%; deprivation negatively associated with some outcomes, partly mitigated | VERIFIED (abstract) | setting/implementation as a cause of variability in CBT outcomes

## Relapse and durability
- Hollon2005Relapse | Hollon 2005 | RCT continuation/follow-up of responders, n=104 (57.8% of those assigned responded) | relapse during continuation 30.8% after CT withdrawal vs 76.2% after medication withdrawal (P=.004) and 47.2% for continued medication (vs CT P=.20) | VERIFIED (abstract) | CBT has enduring effect; still about 1 in 3 relapses within 12 months
- Cuijpers2013Enduring | Cuijpers 2013 | meta-analysis, 9 studies, 506 patients, acute-phase CBT vs pharmacotherapy continued or discontinued, 6-18 months | dropout significantly lower in CBT; relapse OR 2.61 (95% CI 1.58-4.31) favouring CBT vs pharmacotherapy discontinuation; no significant difference vs continued pharmacotherapy; interpret with caution (few studies) | VERIFIED (abstract) | durability and lower dropout vs medication
- Vittengl2007Relapse | Vittengl 2007 | meta-analysis of 28 studies, 1,880 adults, CBT for relapse-recurrence prevention in MDD | after acute-phase CT discontinuation 29% relapse-recur within 1 year and 54% within 2 years; comparable to other depression-specific psychotherapies, lower than pharmacotherapy; continuation-phase CT reduced relapse-recurrence (21% reduction at end of continuation, 29% at follow-up vs assessment only) | VERIFIED (abstract) | relapse rates after CBT and value of continuation CBT
- Biesheuvel2015Recurrence | Biesheuvel-Leliefeld 2015 | meta-analysis and meta-regression, 25 RCTs, preventive psychological interventions for depression | RR of relapse/recurrence 0.64 (95% CI 0.53-0.76) vs treatment as usual | VERIFIED (abstract; only the TAU comparison quoted because the antidepressant comparison text was truncated) | psychological prevention reduces relapse
- Jarrett2001Continuation | Jarrett 2001 | RCT, n=156 recurrent MDD, acute CT then responders randomised to continuation-phase CT vs control | over 8 months relapse 10% vs 31%; early-onset MDD 16% vs 67% over 24 months; unstable late remission 37% vs 62% | VERIFIED (abstract) | high-risk subgroups need continuation CBT
- Fava2004Sixyear | Fava 2004 | RCT follow-up, n=40 recurrent MDD, CBT of residual symptoms vs clinical management after drug taper | 6-year relapse 40% vs 90% | VERIFIED (abstract) | long-term benefit of CBT, small sample
- VanDis2020LongTerm | van Dis 2020 | meta-analysis of 69 RCTs (4118 outpatients), mainly low quality, CBT vs controls in GAD, panic, social anxiety, phobia, PTSD, OCD | effects to 6-12 months for all; at 12+ months still significant for GAD (g 0.22), social anxiety (g 0.42), PTSD (g 0.84), not for panic disorder; relapse 0-14% at 3-12 months but reported in only 6 RCTs | VERIFIED (abstract) | anxiety durability gaps and poor relapse reporting
- Barlow2000Panic | Barlow 2000 | RCT, n=312 panic disorder, CBT vs imipramine vs placebo vs combinations, with 6-month maintenance and 6-month follow-up | acute PDSS response CBT 48.7% vs placebo 21.7% (imipramine 45.8%); after 6 months of maintenance 39.5% CBT vs 13.0% placebo; 6 months after discontinuation CGI response 31.9% CBT alone | VERIFIED (abstract) | CBT effective but response rates under 50% and decline after stopping; "CBT appeared durable"

## Treatment-resistant depression
- Wiles2013CoBalT | Wiles 2013 | RCT, n=469 primary-care TRD (usual care vs CBT plus usual care) | response at 6 months 46% vs 22% (OR 3.26, 95% CI 2.10-5.06) | VERIFIED (abstract) | CBT adjunct works after antidepressant non-response
- Wiles2016CoBalTLong | Wiles 2016 | long-term follow-up of CoBalT, 248 with outcome data, median 45.5 months | BDI-II 19.2 (SD 13.8) CBT vs 23.4 (SD 13.2) usual care; difference in means -4.7 over 46 months; cost-effective | VERIFIED (abstract) | durable but incomplete benefit; attrition caveat (248 of 469)
- Ijaz2018TRD | Ijaz 2018 | Cochrane review, 6 trials, n=698 (3 CBT trials n=522) | adjunctive psychotherapy SMD -0.40 on self-reported depression; remission RR 1.92; no differential dropout (RR 0.85); serious AEs in 4.2% of total sample, only in usual care arm; most medium/long-term evidence from one trial; no evidence on switching to psychotherapy | VERIFIED (abstract) | TRD evidence base is thin
- Thase2007STARD | Thase 2007 | STAR*D second-step, equipoise-stratified RCT (CT augmentation n=65, medication augmentation n=117, switch to CT n=36, switch to medication n=86) | similar response/remission for CT and medication strategies; less than one-third consented to randomisation strata permitting CT vs medication comparison; augmentation with medication faster than CT; switch to CT better tolerated than switch to antidepressant | VERIFIED (abstract) | CBT as second-step; acceptability and limits of generalisation

## Deterioration, negative effects and harm reporting
- Rozental2017Deterioration | Rozental 2017 | IPD meta-analysis, 29 ICBT trials, N=2,866 | deterioration 5.8% (122) in treatment vs 17.4% (130) in controls (OR 3.10 favouring treatment); higher severity, being in a relationship, university degree and older age associated with lower deterioration odds in treatment | VERIFIED (abstract) | deterioration is less likely with CBT than without, but 5.8% still deteriorate
- Ebert2016Deterioration | Ebert 2016 | IPD meta-analysis, 18 studies (21 comparisons), 2079 participants, guided Internet self-help for depression | deterioration 3.36 vs 7.60 (RR 0.47, 95% CI 0.29-0.75); low-education patients at higher risk and no difference from controls; 9.38 patients respond per patient who deteriorates in that group | VERIFIED (abstract) | subgroup with raised risk; benefit-risk ratio
- Crawford2016Negative | Crawford 2016 | cross-sectional survey, 14,587 patients from 184 services in England and Wales | 763 (5.2%) reported lasting bad effects; more likely among sexual and ethnic minorities, those unsure what therapy they had (OR 1.51), fewer if given enough information beforehand (OR 0.65); over-65s less likely | VERIFIED (abstract) | prevalence and correlates of perceived lasting harm (all psychological therapies, not CBT-only)
- Linden2014Rate | Linden 2014 | narrative review, World Psychiatry | "unwanted events should be expected in about 5 to 20% of psychotherapy patients"; reports 21% of RCTs monitored harms (citing Jonsson) | VERIFIED (full text read, PMC4219072; no abstract in indexes) | definition, assessment and rate of side effects
- Linden2012SideEffects | Linden 2013 (online 2012) | conceptual paper | defines unwanted events, adverse treatment reactions, treatment non-response, deterioration, malpractice; proposes UE-ATR checklist | VERIFIED (abstract) | vocabulary distinguishing non-response, deterioration and side effects
- SchermulyHaupt2018Unwanted | Schermuly-Haupt 2018 | interview study, 100 CBT therapists, one current outpatient each | 372 unwanted events in 98 patients; side effects in 43 patients; negative wellbeing/distress 27% of patients, worsening of symptoms 9%, family strains 6%; 21% of patients had severe or very severe and 5% persistent side effects | VERIFIED (abstract, fetched from publisher page); therapist-reported, small sample | side effects frequent even in well-delivered CBT
- Klatte2023Adverse | Klatte 2023 (print 2025) | systematic review, 85 RCTs (126 psychotherapy, 61 control conditions), n=14,420 | harmful events reported in 60% of studies; AEs expected in more than 1 in 10 participants; serious AEs in more than 1 in 21; association to treatment often unclear; recording heterogeneous | VERIFIED (abstract) | quantifies adverse events; reporting inconsistency
- Jonsson2014Harms | Jonsson 2014 | review of 132 psychological-intervention RCTs published 2010 | 28 (21%) monitored harms; 4 (3%) described AEs and methods; 15 (11%) reported only deterioration | VERIFIED (abstract) | harms under-reported
- Duggan2014Adverse | Duggan 2014 | review of 82 NIHR-funded trials 1995-2013 | none of the psychological-intervention trials mentioned an adverse event in their final report; drug-based AE guidelines poorly suited | VERIFIED (abstract) | weak AE recording in psychological trials
- Lilienfeld2007Harm | Lilienfeld 2007 | critical review | argues several psychological treatments may produce harm; outlines obstacles to identifying potentially harmful therapies; no numbers quoted | VERIFIED (abstract) | key critique of harm neglect
- Barlow2010Negative | Barlow 2010 | perspective article (American Psychologist) | methodologies suited to detecting positive effects often obscure negative effects; calls for idiographic approaches; no numbers quoted | VERIFIED (abstract) | key critique from a leading CBT researcher

## Monitoring and mitigation (non-response / deterioration)
- Lambert2018Feedback | Lambert 2018 | meta-analysis, 24 studies of routine outcome monitoring (two feedback systems) | two-thirds of studies favoured ROM-assisted therapy; effects small to moderate; reduced deterioration and nearly doubled clinically significant/reliable change in clients predicted to do poorly | VERIFIED (abstract) | feedback for off-track patients (not CBT-specific)
- Delgadillo2018Feedback | Delgadillo 2018 | cluster RCT, 79 therapists, 2233 patients, English stepped-care services | not-on-track patients had lower post-treatment severity with feedback (PHQ-9 d=0.23, B=-1.03; GAD-7 d=0.19, B=-0.85) | VERIFIED (abstract) | outcome feedback helps patients at risk of poor response
