# Efficacy / bias / head-to-head slice (CBT for anxiety; depression used where anxiety data are thin)

Scope: themes A (efficacy meta-analyses/umbrella reviews), B (decline over time, bias, control conditions), C (head-to-head, component, common factors), D (2020-2026). All DOIs were confirmed against Crossref with bibfetch.py; every number below was read from the abstract (Europe PMC / PubMed) unless marked PARTIAL. Companion file: `/var/lib/agent/scratch/at735/lit/efficacy.bib` (60 entries). Keys follow firstauthor+year+keyword; year in key = print year where Crossref gave an online-first year.

## Synthesis (10 lines)
1. CBT works: against inactive controls it is moderate-to-large (anxiety/depression g ~0.75-0.88 vs mostly waitlist, Cuijpers 2016; 0.56 vs placebo on target symptoms in anxiety, Carpenter 2018).
2. The headline shrinks with better comparators and bias correction: depression psychotherapy g 0.70 unadjusted vs 0.31 adjusted (Cuijpers 2019); umbrella of recent meta-analyses SMD 0.34 vs placebo/TAU (Leichsenring 2022).
3. Anxiety trials lean on waitlists (>80%, Cuijpers 2016); waitlist-controlled g is >0.94 vs 0.22-1.13 for care-as-usual (Cuijpers 2025). Waitlist-as-nocebo is suggestive (Furukawa 2014) but a Cochrane methods review rates the control-condition evidence low/very low (Faltinsen 2022) and CAU categories did not differ significantly (Cuijpers 2021).
4. "Shrinking over time" is only partly supported: the depression decline (Johnsen 2015) was contested and not found in 409 trials (Cuijpers 2023); anxiety shows a flat, not declining, trend (Hofmann 2025: B=-0.008, p=.24), but placebo-controlled anxiety effects fell across successive reviews (g 0.73 in 2008, 0.56 in 2018, 0.24 in a 2022 update dominated by PTSD) -- cross-review comparisons, not one meta-regression.
5. Bias is real and quantified: unpublished NIH-funded trials cut the effect by 25% (Driessen 2015); publication-bias adjustment 0.67 to 0.42 (Cuijpers 2010); researcher allegiance r=.262 (Munder 2013). Still, effects remain positive after adjustment in most analyses.
6. Roughly half respond: anxiety response 49.5% post-treatment (Loerinc 2015), remission 51.0% (Springer 2018); depression response 41% vs 16-17% controls (Cuijpers 2021).
7. CBT is not clearly superior to other bona fide therapies for depression (g=0.06 and mostly n.s. after sensitivity analyses, Cuijpers 2023); in anxiety, CBT does beat psychodynamic/other therapies in social anxiety and is the only GAD therapy with significant long-term effects (Mayo-Wilson 2014; Papola 2024) -- evidence AGAINST a strong "anything works" reading.
8. Component evidence leans toward behavioral ingredients: adding cognitive techniques did not improve outcomes (Jacobson 1996; Longmore & Worrell 2007), BA = CBT (Richards 2016 COBRA; Cuijpers 2007), "pure cognitive restructuring was not a decisively active ingredient" in GAD (Papola 2024), exposure numerically larger than cognitive/behavioral-only in placebo-controlled anxiety trials (Carpenter 2018, n.s.). Counter-evidence: CT > exposure in social phobia (Ougrin 2011); in-session CR correlates with outcome, r=.35 (Ezawa & Hollon 2023, 4 studies).
9. Cognitive change occurs in every effective therapy (Cristea 2015: CBT vs other psychotherapy g=0.17, n.s.) -- consistent with change being a correlate, not proof that persuasion is the engine (Lorenzo-Luaces 2015 reads the same data as supporting cognitive theory).
10. Gaps: none of these reviews tests executive-function load or ADHD directly (ADHD appears only inside the Leichsenring 2022 umbrella); "common factors" are also unproven (Cuijpers 2016/2019), so the dodo-bird reading is not established either.
