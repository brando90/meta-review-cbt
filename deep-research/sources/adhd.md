## Synthesis (10 lines)

1. CBT for adult ADHD has a real but modest evidence base: Knouse 2017 (32 studies) g = 0.65 vs control on self-reported symptoms, with smaller effects against active controls; Young 2020 SMD 0.43 vs active controls; Liu 2026 SMD -0.45 across 14 RCTs.
2. The effect is rater-dependent: in the adult component NMA (Ostinelli 2025) CBT, mindfulness, psychoeducation and cognitive remediation beat placebo only on clinician-rated measures, whereas stimulants and atomoxetine were supported on both self- and clinician-rated scales; Sonuga-Barke 2013 found psychological-treatment effects attenuated to non-significance with probably-blinded ratings (in a mostly-pediatric evidence base; the abstract gives no adult/child split).
3. Medications have the larger and more consistent short-term effect (Cortese 2018 adults, clinician-rated: amphetamines SMD -0.79, methylphenidate -0.49, atomoxetine -0.45), but Cochrane reviews of adult stimulants rate the evidence low or very low, trials are short, and no high-certainty long-term evidence exists for any intervention (Gosling 2025 BMJ umbrella).
4. Combination evidence is mixed rather than clearly favouring CBT: Philipsen 2015 (N=433) found group CBT no better than clinical management but methylphenidate better than placebo; Weiss 2012 found no added benefit of medication on top of CBT (small secondary analysis); Cherkasova 2020 found CBT + medication > CBT alone (unblinded). Kooij 2019 and NICE NG87 position CBT as an adjunct to medication, not a sole treatment.
5. What the successful ADHD CBTs actually teach is mostly behavioural compensation (organising, planning, time management); Knouse and Safren 2010 suggest "targeted learning and practice of specific behavioral compensatory strategies may be a critical active ingredient", and Knouse 2017 reports longer treatments were not better. I found no mediation study isolating the cognitive-restructuring module (gap; supports the thesis only weakly, as absence of evidence).
6. EF deficits in ADHD are real but heterogeneous: Willcutt 2005 meta-analysis (83 studies) effect sizes 0.46-0.69 and "neither necessary nor sufficient"; Kofler (children) found very large central-executive working-memory deficits (d = 1.63-2.03) and 89% impaired on at least one EF. This is consistent with the claim that top-down strategies are costly for this population, but it is evidence about the deficit, not about CBT failing.
7. Anxiety comorbidity is substantial (Hoskins 2026: g = 0.77 for anxiety scores, adults with vs without ADHD) and CBT for ADHD reduces anxiety only modestly (Liu 2026 SMD -0.24; Wang 2026 SMD -0.29 post, -0.42 follow-up); effects partly track core-symptom reduction (Lopez-Pinar 2020).
8. Evidence against a simple version of the thesis: in children with mild-to-moderate ADHD, standard group CBT for anxiety worked as well as in non-ADHD children (Gould 2018, N=842, 94 with ADHD), and anxiety did not bias EF estimates in pediatric ADHD (Marsh 2024). Halldorsdottir and Ollendick 2014 reached a more negative conclusion (PARTIAL: abstract only partly read).
9. Mindfulness (MBCT) shows medium effects (Janssen 2019, d = 0.41, blinded clinician ratings) and a mechanism via self-reported inhibition, not mindfulness skills (Geurts 2021), and does not improve anxiety (Hepark 2019); active-controlled mindfulness effects are low/negligible (Oliva 2021).
10. I found no adult trial of CBT for an anxiety disorder that reports ADHD or EF as a moderator; that question is open for adults and is the main gap relevant to the thesis.
